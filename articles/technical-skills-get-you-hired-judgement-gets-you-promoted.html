Technical Skills Get You Hired. Judgement Gets You Promoted.

Technical skills establish credibility. Judgement earns trust when cybersecurity decisions require context, trade-offs and accountability.

Technical skills are often the first thing employers look for in cybersecurity.

Can you analyse alerts?

Can you configure security controls?

Can you investigate incidents?

Can you review architecture?

Can you understand cloud, identity, networks or applications?

These skills matter.

They help people enter the profession.

They create credibility.

They demonstrate capability.

But technical skills alone do not usually determine how far someone progresses.

At some point, career growth depends less on knowing the answer and more on knowing what decision should be made.

That is where judgement begins to matter.

Technical Knowledge Solves Problems

Cybersecurity professionals are often rewarded early in their careers for solving technical problems.

A vulnerability is found.

An alert is investigated.

A control is implemented.

A configuration is corrected.

A design is reviewed.

The more technical knowledge someone has, the faster and more confidently they can respond.

This is valuable.

But as responsibilities increase, the problems become less clear.

The question is no longer simply:

“Is this secure?”

It becomes:

“Is the remaining risk acceptable given the business objective, timeline, cost and available alternatives?”

That question cannot be answered by technical knowledge alone.

Judgement Begins Where The Framework Ends

Frameworks provide guidance.

Policies define expectations.

Standards establish minimum requirements.

But real situations are rarely perfect.

A project may need to go live before every control is complete.

A critical vulnerability may exist in a system that cannot be patched immediately.

A business unit may request an exception because the approved solution cannot support an important process.

A security control may reduce risk while creating significant operational disruption.

The framework may tell you what should happen.

Judgement helps determine what should happen next.

This does not mean ignoring standards.

It means understanding how to apply them responsibly when the situation is complex.

Senior Roles Require Trade-Offs

As cybersecurity professionals become more senior, they are expected to balance competing priorities.

Security.

Operations.

Cost.

Time.

Customer impact.

Regulatory expectations.

Business value.

A technically ideal solution may be too expensive.

A fast solution may create unacceptable risk.

A secure design may introduce operational complexity that the organisation cannot sustain.

Senior professionals must recognise these trade-offs and explain them clearly.

They must recommend a path forward.

Not simply identify what is wrong.

That is the difference between reporting a problem and leading a decision.

Good Judgement Is Built Through Experience

Judgement is difficult to teach through certification alone.

It develops through exposure.

Working through incidents.

Observing failed projects.

Reviewing difficult exceptions.

Seeing how technical decisions affect the business.

Learning when to challenge.

Learning when to compromise.

Learning when to escalate.

Learning when a risk is manageable—and when it is not.

Experience does not automatically create good judgement.

People must reflect on what happened, why it happened and what they would do differently next time.

Without reflection, experience becomes repetition.

With reflection, it becomes judgement.

Knowing When To Say No Is Not Enough

Cybersecurity professionals are often praised for being willing to challenge weak decisions.

That is important.

But leadership requires more than saying no.

A strong professional should also be able to explain:

  • Why the risk matters
  • What the likely business impact is
  • Which alternatives are available
  • What compensating controls could reduce exposure
  • What decision is recommended
  • Who should accept the remaining risk

Anyone can identify a problem.

More senior roles require people who can help the organisation move forward safely.

Good judgement is not about blocking decisions.

It is about improving them.

Communication Is Part Of Judgement

A good decision poorly communicated may still fail.

Senior cybersecurity professionals must adapt their message to the audience.

Engineers may need technical detail.

Management may need risk, cost and business impact.

Executives may need a clear recommendation and an understanding of the consequences.

Judgement includes knowing what matters to each audience.

It also includes knowing what does not need to be said.

The objective is not to demonstrate how much you know.

It is to help others make a better decision.

Promotion Changes The Questions You Must Answer

Early in a career, people are often asked:

“Can you perform this task?”

Later, the questions change.

“What do you recommend?”

“What is the risk if we proceed?”

“Which option should we choose?”

“Can the organisation operate safely with this exception?”

“What happens if this control fails?”

These questions require confidence, context and accountability.

They also require the willingness to make a recommendation without having perfect information.

That is one of the hardest transitions in any professional career.

Technical experts are trained to look for certainty.

Leaders often have to make decisions while uncertainty remains.

Final Thoughts

Technical skills remain essential.

They establish credibility.

They help professionals understand how systems work, how threats operate and how controls reduce risk.

But career progression eventually requires something more.

The ability to understand context.

Balance competing priorities.

Recognise consequences.

Communicate trade-offs.

And make responsible decisions when the answer is not obvious.

Technical skills may help you secure the opportunity.

Judgement determines whether people trust you with greater responsibility.

Because promotion is rarely about knowing more than everyone else.

It is about demonstrating that you can make sound decisions when the situation becomes difficult.

Question assumptions. Share knowledge. Build trust.
